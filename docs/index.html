A few useful scientific words, in plain English

Baseline: the ordinary method your idea must beat. Hypothesis: an explanation you can test. Falsify: find a result that shows a claim is wrong. Preregister: write down the rules and success measure before seeing results. Held-out cases: examples you save for a final test instead of using them to tune the method. Calibration: whether predicted chances match what actually happens. Uncertainty interval: a range showing how much an estimate could vary under stated statistical assumptions. Constraint: a limit the decision must obey.

FIELD NOTE 01 / Cooperation

LOVE Protocol

Reported simulation · validation pending

Two neighbors share a garden. Forgive an accidental broken tool, but set boundaries if one neighbor repeatedly takes the harvest.

The real question. When should an agent cooperate, punish or forgive in repeated encounters?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

The archived design describes an eight-action trust window, tolerance for occasional defections and a conditional forgiveness branch. Actions are cooperate or defect. Track each player’s payoff and cooperation separately; a policy can improve one while harming the other.

  1. Specify actions, payoff rules and forgiveness exactly.
  2. Replay equal-length encounters with matched random seeds.
  3. Compare cooperation, individual payoff and exploitability separately.
c = N(C) / N; π = Σ rₜ / N
c
Fraction of cooperative actions (0–1)
N(C)
Number of cooperative actions
N
Total actions or rounds in the relevant measure
π
Mean payoff per round, in game units
rₜ
Payoff earned in round t

02 / Work through the numbers

Teaching example, not a LOVE run: payoffs 3, 3, 0, 5 give π = 11 / 4 = 2.75 game units. If 3 of 4 actions cooperate, c = 75%. The two measures answer different questions.

03 / What has been achieved

The original website documents a synthetic cooperation/payoff trade-off: more cooperation accompanied lower individual payoff. Its exact outputs have not been reproduced for this release.

What remains unverified

Original synthetic scripts, seeds and raw outputs are absent here. The newer unfavorable evolutionary outcome is user-reported and awaits original-source inspection and independent reruns.

04 / Where it could fail

An opponent can exploit predictable forgiveness. Cooperation is not an evolutionary stability proof.

05 / The economic value channel

Potentially fewer disputes and failed partnerships. Measure avoided incident cost minus monitoring, concessions and implementation costs; never convert game points directly to dollars.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Obtain the original archive; freeze policies, payoff matrix and noise; run matched seeds against cooperative, retaliating and exploitative opponents, including invasion tests.

Baseline: Tit-for-Tat, always defect and a strong forgiving baseline, with identical noise and opponents.

Measure: Paired mean payoff, cooperation fraction, worst-opponent loss and uncertainty across seeds.

Stop or reject: Reject an advantage claim if the confidence interval for payoff improvement includes zero or adversarial losses exceed a preregistered safety limit.

FIELD NOTE 02 / Cooperation

Redemption Amplifier

Proposed · validation pending

A library restores borrowing privileges after reliable behavior, while preventing someone from repeatedly cheating and resetting their record.

The real question. How much evidence should restore trust after a violation?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

A candidate rule subtracts a severity penalty from a bounded trust score, then rewards verified reliable actions. A restoration multiplier is a tunable hypothesis, not a proven fairness mechanism.

  1. Define the violation and what counts as recovery evidence.
  2. Apply bounded penalties and controlled restoration.
  3. Test whether repeated cheating becomes more attractive.
T′ = min(1, max(0, T − s + k·r))
T, T′
Trust score before and after a review (0–1)
s
Severity penalty in score units
k
Restoration multiplier, nonnegative
r
Verified recovery evidence in score units

02 / Work through the numbers

Illustrative rule: T = 0.4, s = 0.2, k = 1.5 and r = 0.1 give T′ = 0.35. A larger multiplier restores privileges faster but can also help repeat offenders.

03 / What has been achieved

The archive describes a multiplier concept and a separate simplified ablation claim. They must not be treated as the same tested algorithm.

What remains unverified

No supplied run establishes that the multiplier reduces exploitation or improves fairness. The toy rule here only explains the mechanism.

04 / Where it could fail

Alternating misconduct and minimum recovery can become a profitable strategy.

05 / The economic value channel

Compare avoided false exclusions against repeat fraud, review labor and reinstatement costs. Recovery speed alone is not economic success.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Freeze this candidate rule and simulate repeated offenders alongside genuinely reformed agents; vary multiplier and observation error.

Baseline: Permanent exclusion, fixed probation and recovery without amplification.

Measure: Repeat-offense loss, false exclusion rate and median time to safe restoration.

Stop or reject: Reject if faster recovery raises total loss or false exclusion beyond a declared tolerance.

FIELD NOTE 03 / AI systems

BioSynth.AI

Proposed · validation pending

A librarian gathers evidence, a scientist proposes an explanation and a skeptic checks it. They all leave notes you can inspect.

The real question. Can automated research produce more supported answers per hour?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

Keep a claim ledger with source passages, alternative explanations and executable checks. A verifier marks unsupported claims and a human resolves conflicts before publication.

  1. Attach every factual claim to a source passage.
  2. Generate a rival explanation and a check that could distinguish it.
  3. Publish accepted results with unresolved claims still visible.
q = S / C; cost_correct = K / V
q
Supported-claim fraction
S
Claims supported by inspected sources
C
Total factual claims reviewed
K
Total research and review cost in dollars
V
Independently accepted correct answers

02 / Work through the numbers

If 18 of 20 checked claims are supported, q = 90%. If a workflow costs $60 and produces 15 accepted answers, cost_correct = $4. Neither number is a measured BioSynth result.

03 / What has been achieved

This repository implements an educational website and arithmetic demos. The earlier site describes recovered research components, but does not include them.

What remains unverified

An end-to-end autonomous research engine and a measured accuracy advantage are not established by this checkout.

04 / Where it could fail

Agents can repeat the same unsupported claim; many citations can still fail to support the conclusion.

05 / The economic value channel

Measure cost per accepted answer, including source retrieval, inference, correction and reviewer time. Count work only after verification.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Create a frozen set of answerable research questions and source snapshots; blind reviewers to the workflow used.

Baseline: One model with retrieval and the same time, sources and reviewer budget.

Measure: Source support, correct answers, correction minutes and full dollars per accepted answer.

Stop or reject: Reject superiority if quality does not improve at equal total cost, or if hidden review labor explains the gain.

FIELD NOTE 04 / Decision science

APEX

Proposed · validation pending

A coach chooses the best training plan that fits the budget, schedule and safety rules.

The real question. Which feasible decision offers the most value under real constraints?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

Define a measurable objective, enumerate feasible decisions and choose the highest-value one. Hard limits constrain the choice; arbitrary weighted scores require explicit justification.

  1. Choose the outcome and units before scoring candidates.
  2. Remove decisions that violate budget or safety limits.
  3. Compare the best feasible decision with a simple baseline.
maxₓ V(x), subject to C(x) ≤ B and H(x) ≤ L
x
A candidate decision or resource allocation
V(x)
Value of the decision in declared units
C(x)
Cost of the decision
B
Available budget
H(x), L
Resource usage and its hard limit

02 / Work through the numbers

Plan A costs $80 and has modeled benefit $120; plan B costs $110 and benefit $170. With budget B = $100, only A is feasible. Its modeled net is $40, not the $60 of infeasible B.

03 / What has been achieved

A conceptual optimization framework is described. Constrained optimization is established mathematics; naming a framework does not establish a new theorem.

What remains unverified

No supplied comparative benchmark shows APEX beats existing decision rules.

04 / Where it could fail

Incorrect objectives optimize the wrong thing; inconsistent units make weighted rankings meaningless.

05 / The economic value channel

Incremental realized net value relative to the best feasible alternative, including decision and oversight costs.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Choose one scheduling or procurement dataset; preregister objective and constraints, then compare on held-out cases.

Baseline: Existing practice, a simple greedy rule and a conventional optimization solver.

Measure: Feasible net value, constraint violations and decision time.

Stop or reject: Reject if a simpler baseline matches value at lower cost or any hard safety constraint is violated.

FIELD NOTE 05 / Decision science

FORGE

Source inspected · validation pending

A bridge closure can send traffic pressure through many neighborhoods. A supplier disruption can spread through a company network.

The real question. Where might semiconductor supply-chain disruption create indirect exposure?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

A directed graph connects pressure sources to exposed targets. Normalize incoming edge weights into matrix P, inject scenario b and iterate a damped propagation rule. Scores are illustrative unsigned exposures.

  1. Document each relationship and separate assumed weights.
  2. Propagate a declared disruption through a bounded network.
  3. Check sensitivity and compare historical event rankings.
x(t+1) = (1 − d)b + dP x(t)
x(t)
Vector of illustrative exposure scores at iteration t
b
Scenario source vector, in score units
d
Continuation parameter (0–0.95)
P
Nonnegative matrix with every row sum at most 1
t
Iteration index

02 / Work through the numbers

Two-node toy: b = (1, 0), d = 0.5 and P₂₁ = 0.8, with all other entries zero. The fixed point is x₁ = 0.5 and x₂ = 0.2. A score of 0.2 is neither 20% loss nor a 20% probability.

03 / What has been achieved

Public MODEL.md was inspected. It specifies normalization, convergence bounds and the distinction between relationship evidence and assumed coefficients.

What remains unverified

The external engine was not executed in this release. Edge weights are not calibrated loss estimates or validated stock forecasts.

04 / Where it could fail

A disclosed supplier relationship does not reveal dependence, substitution or inventory buffers.

05 / The economic value channel

Prioritize due diligence and resilience analysis. Measure whether scenario-guided decisions reduce disruption costs compared with ordinary review.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Freeze pre-event graphs and compare exposure rankings around documented disruptions, without using facts published after each event.

Baseline: Direct-supplier-only exposure and simple industry concentration rankings.

Measure: Rank calibration and decision usefulness across held-out historical events.

Stop or reject: Reject predictive claims if rankings fail to improve on the direct-only baseline; do not infer dollar losses without calibration.

FIELD NOTE 06 / Operations

Q-Branch Purchasing

Source inspected · validation pending

A $75 item arriving after your trip can cost more in lost use than a $90 item ready today.

The real question. How should price, delivery time and uncertainty influence a purchase?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

Add acquisition costs to the opportunity cost of waiting. Treat delivery probability as an assumption to calibrate. Missing arrival information is unknown risk, never evidence of safe delivery.

  1. List all cash costs and the value of earlier use.
  2. Estimate arrival uncertainty without filling unknowns with zero.
  3. Compare total economic cost and audit actual outcomes.
C = P + S + T + R + V + d·u
C
Total economic acquisition cost ($)
P, S, T
Price, shipping and tax ($)
R, V
Expected repair and pickup travel cost ($)
d
Days until usable
u
Value of earlier use ($/day)

02 / Work through the numbers

With other costs set to zero, shipped option costs $75 + 5 days × $4/day = $95. Local pickup costs $90 + 0 = $90. Local has $5 lower modeled economic cost, not $5 proven cash savings.

03 / What has been achieved

Public Python source was inspected: it implements acquisition cost and a delivery-window assumption.

What remains unverified

Actual purchasing improvements and delivery calibration remain unverified. The source defaults unknown late risk to zero; users must not interpret that as certainty.

04 / Where it could fail

Assumed delivery windows and daily value can dominate the ranking. A point estimate hides uncertainty.

05 / The economic value channel

Compare missed deadlines, expediting, repair costs and user-valued waiting against the price-only choice.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Collect de-identified purchases with predicted and actual arrival dates; evaluate rankings and deadline probabilities.

Baseline: Lowest posted price and a simple deadline-screened price rule.

Measure: Total observed cost, deadline misses and probability calibration.

Stop or reject: Reject if adjusted choices do not reduce held-out cost or deadline misses relative to simple rules.

FIELD NOTE 07 / Decision science

OTSA

Proposed · validation pending

An investing GPS must include tolls, rough roads and taxes when comparing routes.

The real question. Can an investment policy improve after-cost, after-tax outcomes at comparable risk?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

Proposed regime estimation and portfolio rules select trades under risk and tax-lot constraints. Tax-aware means keeping track of the acquisition cost and holding period of each lot; no current tax rate is assumed here.

  1. Freeze the strategy before viewing the evaluation period.
  2. Apply fees, execution frictions and declared tax assumptions.
  3. Compare net results at similar risk and report losing periods.
G_net = G_gross − F − S − T
G_net, G_gross
Net and gross investment gain ($)
F
Fees ($)
S
Slippage: execution cost relative to the assumed price ($)
T
Modeled tax cost ($), supplied as a scenario

02 / Work through the numbers

Illustrative arithmetic: $1,000 gross gain − $80 fees − $70 execution cost − $200 hypothetical tax = $650 net gain. This says nothing about OTSA profitability or current tax treatment.

03 / What has been achieved

An investment research design is described in the archive. No verified trading record is supplied.

What remains unverified

Profitability, generalization, tax correctness and out-of-sample performance are unverified.

04 / Where it could fail

Future information leakage, repeated tuning and unrealistic fills can create fictional backtest profits.

05 / The economic value channel

Compare after-cost, after-tax outcomes and drawdowns at matched risk. Include data, compute, turnover and review costs.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Freeze strategy and point-in-time dataset; retain a genuinely untouched test interval and run a cost-sensitive walk-forward evaluation.

Baseline: Low-turnover benchmark portfolio under the same cost and tax assumptions.

Measure: Net return, drawdown, turnover, benchmark difference and uncertainty.

Stop or reject: Reject superiority if held-out advantage disappears under realistic cost sensitivity or cannot survive correction for repeated trials.

FIELD NOTE 08 / AI systems

Quantum-Inspired Q-Branch

Proposed · validation pending

A detective keeps several explanations open and changes their confidence when new evidence arrives.

The real question. Can considering competing hypotheses improve decisions under uncertainty?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

Generate rival explanations, collect distinguishing evidence and update beliefs. This lesson uses ordinary Bayesian probability on classical hardware; it does not implement quantum computation.

  1. Keep distinct explanations and explicit initial probabilities.
  2. Seek evidence that would differ under those explanations.
  3. Update confidence and retain unresolved uncertainty.
P(A|E) = p·a / (p·a + (1 − p)·b)
A, E
Candidate explanation and observed evidence
p
Prior probability P(A)
a
Likelihood P(E|A)
b
Likelihood P(E|not A)
P(A|E)
Updated probability after evidence

02 / Work through the numbers

With prior p = 0.4, a = 0.8 and b = 0.2, the updated probability is 0.32 / 0.44 ≈ 72.7%. These are assumed probabilities, not measured model confidence.

03 / What has been achieved

A conceptual architecture is described; this release provides a classical probability teaching calculator.

What remains unverified

No quantum speedup, quantum mechanism or accuracy advantage over strong classical methods is established.

04 / Where it could fail

Correlated branches repeat mistakes; guessed likelihoods can create false confidence.

05 / The economic value channel

Value equals avoided decision loss minus additional inference and review cost, measured at matched budgets.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Test a frozen set of verifiable tasks using single-path and multi-hypothesis methods at equal total inference and review budgets.

Baseline: Single-path reasoning and a strong classical search or planning baseline.

Measure: Correct answers per dollar, calibration and abstention quality.

Stop or reject: Reject if improvements vanish at equal budget or confidence becomes less calibrated.

FIELD NOTE 09 / Cooperation

Multi-Agent Evolution

Proposed · validation pending

A village changes when successful behaviors spread, even if one friendly pair gets along well.

The real question. Which strategies survive competition, mutation and invasion?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

Track strategy shares over generations. The teaching demo uses two-strategy discrete replicator selection: shares rise when expected payoff exceeds the population average. It is not a LOVE experiment.

  1. Specify the population, payoff matrix and selection rule.
  2. Run invasion and mutation conditions across many seeds.
  3. Separate pairwise success from population survival.
p′ = p·f_A / (p·f_A + (1 − p)·f_B)
p, p′
Share of strategy A before and after selection
f_A
A expected payoff, 4p in this toy coordination game
f_B
B expected payoff, fixed at 3
4, 3
Assumed game payoffs, not measured economic values

02 / Work through the numbers

At p = 0.6, f_A = 2.4 and f_B = 3. Average payoff is 2.76; p′ = 1.44 / 2.76 ≈ 0.522. A shrinks. It grows only above p = 0.75 under these specific rules.

03 / What has been achieved

This release implements a deterministic teaching recurrence. Archived historical population claims have not been independently reproduced.

What remains unverified

The described newer unfavorable LOVE study requires the original repository, algorithm versions, seeds and outputs before its result can be verified.

04 / Where it could fail

Changing payoff rules, mutation or update mechanics changes the outcome. A curated opponent panel cannot establish invasion resistance.

05 / The economic value channel

Explore the durability of cooperative institutions. Game payoffs remain game units; an operational value claim needs a separate field comparison.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Obtain original LOVE evolution materials; preregister mixed populations, rare-invader tests, selection rules, noise and seed panels.

Baseline: Always defect, Tit-for-Tat and forgiving competitors under the identical selection process.

Measure: Invasion success, fixation frequency, population payoff and cooperation over seeds.

Stop or reject: Reject stability if a rare competitor reliably invades across the preregistered conditions.

FIELD NOTE 10 / Ethics

Dignity Optimization

Proposed · validation pending

A school cannot improve average scores by excluding children who need help. Rights constrain the decision.

The real question. How can resource allocation improve outcomes while respecting rights?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

Optimize a declared service outcome subject to justified minimum access, consent and safety constraints. Human dignity is a boundary on permissible decisions, not a scalar measure of human worth.

  1. Agree on rights and minimum access with affected people.
  2. Check whether the budget can satisfy those constraints.
  3. Compare outcomes only among ethically feasible options.
max Σ uᵢxᵢ, subject to Σ cᵢxᵢ ≤ B and xᵢ ≥ mᵢ
xᵢ
Service units allocated to group i
uᵢ
Declared service outcome per unit, not human worth
cᵢ
Cost per service unit ($)
B
Available budget ($)
mᵢ
Justified minimum service allocation

02 / Work through the numbers

Two groups need at least 3 sessions each. At $10 a session and budget $80, the first 6 sessions cost $60, leaving $20 for 2 additional sessions. The minimum comes from a rights process, not the equation.

03 / What has been achieved

A rights-constrained allocation proposal is documented. No ethical or clinical validation is supplied.

What remains unverified

Consent, proxy fairness and real distributive effects need participatory evaluation before deployment.

04 / Where it could fail

Unjustified minimums or biased outcome measures can encode discrimination in a precise-looking model.

05 / The economic value channel

Compare service outcomes and budget use while preserving rights. Do not assign a dollar price to dignity.

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Co-design constraints with affected groups; test adversarial cases, infeasible budgets and disparate access before any pilot.

Baseline: Current allocation and a rights-preserving rule with no optimization.

Measure: Access gaps, rights violations, service outcomes and feasibility.

Stop or reject: Reject any policy violating agreed rights; when the budget is infeasible, report infeasibility rather than silently relaxing rights.

A restaurant team can divide jobs well, but making four people check every sandwich may cost more than it saves.

  1. Classify tasks, permissions and where information may flow.
  2. Route to a model and verify output before tool execution.

FIELD NOTE 12 / Operations

Freight RFQ Optimization

Proposed · validation pending

A careful assistant copies carrier quotes into the same comparison sheet, flags uncertain numbers and waits for the decision-maker to approve.

The real question. Can quotations be processed faster without changing approval rights?

Open the lesson: mechanism, mathematics & evidence

01 / Follow the mechanism

Extract fields from email or PDF into one master Excel workbook. Logistics checks ambiguous fields and sends the comparison to the business unit. The business unit chooses; logistics books only after a recorded approval.

  1. Extract and flag uncertain fields in the existing master workbook.
  2. Have logistics review and the business unit approve a carrier.
  3. Book only after approval; log time, corrections and outcomes.

Workflow design and an interactive capacity calculator are implemented in this educational release. No freight extraction engine or production booking system is included.

04 / Where it could fail

06 / The next falsifiable experiment

Use de-identified or synthetic quotations; time manual and assisted handling on matched cases and audit every field and approval transition.

Baseline: The existing one-master-workbook process with the same business-unit approval requirement.

02 / SCIENTIFIC LABORATORY

Make the claim
possible to disprove.

A good experiment can tell you that your favorite idea is wrong. Define the comparison before seeing the outcome.

  1. 01 AskOne clear, answerable question.

    Freight example: can assisted extraction reduce handling time without increasing critical errors?

  2. 02 ModelInputs, rules, outputs and assumptions.

    Record monthly volume, human minutes, labor cost, realization and full operating and development cost.

  3. 03 TestA result that could falsify the claim.

    If handling time does not fall or critical errors rise, reject the efficiency claim.

  4. 04 CompareA meaningful baseline, equal resources.

TEACHING MODEL / EVOLUTION

Will cooperation spread?

In this toy coordination game, strategy A earns 4p and B earns 3. A grows only when its share p exceeds 75%.

Assumptions: infinite deterministic population, no mutation, discrete selection. At 60%, the next share is about 52.2%. This is not a reproduction of LOVE.

Let evidence change your mind.

Two explanations compete. Assume evidence is 80% likely if A is true, and 20% likely if A is false.

With a 40% prior, Bayes’ rule gives about 72.7% afterward. The likelihoods are assumptions. This is classical probability, with no quantum hardware or speedup.

05 / IMPLEMENTATION ROADMAP

The next useful step
is a specific experiment.

Proposed sequence, not promised delivery dates. Each system has its own deliverable and a reason to stop if the evidence fails.

FIRST

Recover & specify

Obtain original source, version claims, define metrics and lock baselines.

NEXT

Compare & challenge

Run held-out cases and adversarial tests. Include full cost and uncertainty.

THEN

Reproduce & pilot

Invite independent reruns. Pilot only with permission, review and rollback.

01 LOVE Protocol

Next action: Obtain the original archive; freeze policies, payoff matrix and noise; run matched seeds against cooperative, retaliating and exploitative opponents, including invasion tests.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Paired mean payoff, cooperation fraction, worst-opponent loss and uncertainty across seeds.

Acceptance gate: Reject an advantage claim if the confidence interval for payoff improvement includes zero or adversarial losses exceed a preregistered safety limit.

02 Redemption Amplifier

Next action: Freeze this candidate rule and simulate repeated offenders alongside genuinely reformed agents; vary multiplier and observation error.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Repeat-offense loss, false exclusion rate and median time to safe restoration.

Acceptance gate: Reject if faster recovery raises total loss or false exclusion beyond a declared tolerance.

03 BioSynth.AI

Next action: Create a frozen set of answerable research questions and source snapshots; blind reviewers to the workflow used.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Source support, correct answers, correction minutes and full dollars per accepted answer.

Acceptance gate: Reject superiority if quality does not improve at equal total cost, or if hidden review labor explains the gain.

04 APEX

Next action: Choose one scheduling or procurement dataset; preregister objective and constraints, then compare on held-out cases.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Feasible net value, constraint violations and decision time.

Acceptance gate: Reject if a simpler baseline matches value at lower cost or any hard safety constraint is violated.

05 FORGE

Next action: Freeze pre-event graphs and compare exposure rankings around documented disruptions, without using facts published after each event.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Rank calibration and decision usefulness across held-out historical events.

Acceptance gate: Reject predictive claims if rankings fail to improve on the direct-only baseline; do not infer dollar losses without calibration.

06 Q-Branch Purchasing

Next action: Collect de-identified purchases with predicted and actual arrival dates; evaluate rankings and deadline probabilities.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Total observed cost, deadline misses and probability calibration.

Acceptance gate: Reject if adjusted choices do not reduce held-out cost or deadline misses relative to simple rules.

07 OTSA

Next action: Freeze strategy and point-in-time dataset; retain a genuinely untouched test interval and run a cost-sensitive walk-forward evaluation.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Net return, drawdown, turnover, benchmark difference and uncertainty.

Acceptance gate: Reject superiority if held-out advantage disappears under realistic cost sensitivity or cannot survive correction for repeated trials.

08 Quantum-Inspired Q-Branch

Next action: Test a frozen set of verifiable tasks using single-path and multi-hypothesis methods at equal total inference and review budgets.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Correct answers per dollar, calibration and abstention quality.

Acceptance gate: Reject if improvements vanish at equal budget or confidence becomes less calibrated.

09 Multi-Agent Evolution

Next action: Obtain original LOVE evolution materials; preregister mixed populations, rare-invader tests, selection rules, noise and seed panels.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Invasion success, fixation frequency, population payoff and cooperation over seeds.

Acceptance gate: Reject stability if a rare competitor reliably invades across the preregistered conditions.

10 Dignity Optimization

Next action: Co-design constraints with affected groups; test adversarial cases, infeasible budgets and disparate access before any pilot.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Access gaps, rights violations, service outcomes and feasibility.

Acceptance gate: Reject any policy violating agreed rights; when the budget is infeasible, report infeasibility rather than silently relaxing rights.

11 Autonomous AI Systems

Next action: Use synthetic documents and a fixed task suite; compare single-model, routed-agent and human-assisted workflows with identical acceptance criteria.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Accepted completions, error severity, human correction time, latency and full cost.

Acceptance gate: Reject claimed efficiency if review cost or serious error rates offset the throughput gain.

12 Freight RFQ Optimization

Next action: Use de-identified or synthetic quotations; time manual and assisted handling on matched cases and audit every field and approval transition.

Deliverable: Versioned method, baseline comparison and raw result table for: Human minutes per quote, exact field accuracy, corrections, approval completeness and realized cost changes.

Acceptance gate: Reject deployment if critical-field accuracy or approval compliance is below the agreed threshold, even when average processing is faster.
